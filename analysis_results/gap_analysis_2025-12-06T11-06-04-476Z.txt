# Compliance Executive Summary

The organization faces **three critical regulatory gaps** under Malaysia's AMLA 2001 that require immediate remediation. Most critically, there is **no transaction monitoring or suspicious activity reporting (STR) infrastructure**, leaving the institution unable to fulfill its mandatory obligation to report transactions suspected of involving unlawful proceeds to BNM's FIU—a fundamental AML requirement. Additionally, the compliance framework addresses customer onboarding but **lacks transaction threshold-based CDD triggers and monitoring mechanisms**, creating exposure for transactions exceeding prescribed limits. Finally, while record retention obligations are acknowledged, **no operational procedures, storage standards, or automated controls exist** to ensure systematic creation, protection, and retrieval of compliant records over the required 6-year period.

**Recommended actions**: (1) Immediately implement transaction monitoring and STR capabilities with defined escalation procedures to BNM; (2) Establish transaction threshold monitoring controls tied to CDD requirements; (3) Deploy systematic record management infrastructure with documented procedures, access controls, and audit trails. Without these foundational controls, the institution faces significant regulatory and legal risk.

1. 🔴 [CRITICAL] Suspicious Transaction Reporting
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   
   Gap Description:
   The regulatory obligation requires 'prompt reporting of any transaction that gives reason to suspect it involves proceeds of an unlawful activity.' The current implementation has critical gaps: (1) No transaction monitoring or anomaly detection system is described in the product specification; (2) No suspicious transaction reporting (STR) procedure exists in the internal policy; (3) The product's risk scoring is limited to 'customer profiles based on CDD data and sanctions lists' and does not extend to transaction-level analysis; (4) No defined reporting mechanism, timeline, or escalation procedure to BNM's Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU) is documented; (5) No criteria for determining what constitutes a 'reason to suspect' unlawful activity in transaction context; (6) No record-keeping requirements specific to STR submissions are mentioned in the policy's record retention section.
   
   Current State:
   The product specification (Veris Compliance Engine) provides automated regulatory scanning, policy gap analysis, and real-time risk scoring based on CDD data and sanctions lists. The internal policy (Standard CDD Policy) focuses on customer identification, verification, and sanctions screening with daily batch screening and risk rating assignment. However, neither the product nor the policy contains any mechanism for detecting, flagging, or reporting suspicious transactions as required by Section 14(1) of AMLA 2001.
   
   Regulatory Citation:
   Anti-Money Laundering, Anti-Terrorism Financing and Proceeds of Unlawful Activities Act 2001, Section 14(1)
   
   Risk Assessment:
   • Regulatory Risk: Non-compliance with Section 14(1) of AMLA 2001 constitutes a direct violation of the primary AML/CFT reporting obligation. BNM and the Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU) conduct regular compliance examinations specifically for STR reporting adequacy. Failure to report suspicious transactions exposes the institution to regulatory enforcement action, including cease-and-desist orders, license suspension, or revocation. The institution may also be designated as non-compliant with international AML/CFT standards (FATF Recommendation 20), affecting correspondent banking relationships and international reputation.
   • Financial Impact: Under AMLA 2001, Section 14 violations can result in fines up to RM500,000 and/or imprisonment for responsible officers. Additionally, BNM can impose administrative penalties ranging from RM50,000 to RM1,000,000+ depending on severity and breach history. Reputational damage may lead to loss of correspondent banking relationships, increased compliance costs for remediation, potential civil litigation from affected parties, and mandatory implementation of enhanced monitoring systems. Cost of STR system implementation and staff retraining could exceed RM2-5 million depending on transaction volume.
   • Operational Impact: Without transaction monitoring capabilities, the institution cannot fulfill its core AML/CFT obligation, creating operational chaos during regulatory examinations. Staff lack clear procedures for identifying and reporting suspicious transactions, leading to inconsistent decision-making and potential missed reporting deadlines. The absence of defined escalation procedures means suspicious transactions may be processed without compliance review. Lack of audit trails for STR decisions creates evidentiary gaps during regulatory inspections. The institution operates in a state of continuous non-compliance, with each unreported suspicious transaction creating additional regulatory exposure.
   
   Recommendation:
   Immediately implement a comprehensive transaction monitoring and suspicious transaction reporting capability. This requires: (1) Enhancement of the Veris Compliance Engine to include transaction-level monitoring with behavioral analytics and rule-based detection; (2) Development of a formal Suspicious Transaction Reporting Policy aligned with BNM guidelines and AMLA 2001 Section 14 requirements; (3) Integration with the FIU's reporting portal for timely STR submissions; (4) Establishment of clear criteria for 'reason to suspect' based on transaction characteristics, customer risk profile, and contextual factors; (5) Implementation of mandatory staff training on STR obligations; (6) Creation of audit trails documenting all STR decisions and reporting actions.
   
   Remediation Steps:
      1. Conduct immediate gap assessment of current transaction monitoring capabilities against BNM's Transaction Monitoring Guidelines and AMLA 2001 Section 14 requirements
   2. Develop a formal Suspicious Transaction Reporting Policy defining: (a) transaction monitoring scope and frequency, (b) suspicious activity indicators and detection rules, (c) escalation procedures and timelines, (d) reporting requirements to FIU, (e) documentation and record-keeping requirements (minimum 6 years), (f) staff roles and responsibilities
   3. Enhance Veris Compliance Engine to include: (a) real-time transaction monitoring module with rule-based and behavioral analytics, (b) transaction risk scoring incorporating amount, frequency, counterparty, geographic factors, and customer risk profile, (c) automated flagging of transactions exceeding defined thresholds or matching suspicious patterns, (d) integration with FIU's STR reporting system
   4. Define specific suspicious activity indicators aligned with AMLA 2001 and BNM guidance, including: structuring/smurfing, round-tripping, unusual geographic flows, high-risk jurisdiction transactions, layering patterns, and contextual red flags inconsistent with customer profile
   5. Establish mandatory reporting timeline: suspicious transactions must be reported to FIU within 3 business days of detection (or as per BNM directive), with expedited reporting for terrorism financing suspicions
   6. Implement audit trail functionality capturing: transaction details, detection methodology, risk scoring rationale, reviewer identity, approval/rejection decision, reporting timestamp, and FIU reference number
   7. Develop and deliver mandatory AML/CFT training to all staff involved in transaction processing, compliance review, and reporting, with specific focus on STR obligations and indicators
   8. Establish internal escalation procedure: frontline staff → Compliance Officer → Head of Compliance → FIU reporting, with defined decision points and approval authorities
   9. Update record-keeping policy to explicitly include STR documentation: flagged transaction details, analysis supporting suspicion, internal communications, reporting confirmation from FIU, and retention for minimum 6 years
   10. Conduct regulatory readiness assessment with BNM to validate STR implementation against examination standards before full deployment
   11. Implement quarterly testing and validation of transaction monitoring rules to ensure effectiveness and reduce false positives

2. 🟠 [HIGH] Customer Due Diligence (CDD)
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   
   Gap Description:
   Section 16(1) AMLA 2001 mandates CDD when: (a) establishing business relations OR (b) when a transaction exceeds the prescribed limit. The current internal policy comprehensively covers scenario (a) through the 'Individual Customer Onboarding' procedure, but contains no documented procedures, controls, or monitoring mechanisms for scenario (b) - transaction threshold-based CDD triggers. The policy lacks: (1) definition of the prescribed transaction limit, (2) real-time transaction monitoring logic, (3) procedures for enhanced CDD when thresholds are exceeded, (4) escalation workflows for threshold breaches, and (5) record-keeping requirements specific to threshold-triggered CDD events. The Veris Engine's 'Real-time Risk Scoring' feature operates on customer profiles and sanctions data but does not explicitly incorporate transaction amount thresholds as CDD triggers.
   
   Current State:
   The internal CDD policy addresses core KYC verification and sanctions screening for individual customer onboarding. The Veris Compliance Engine provides automated regulatory scanning, policy gap analysis, and risk scoring capabilities. However, the policy focuses primarily on initial account establishment procedures and does not explicitly address the transaction threshold monitoring requirement stipulated in Section 16(1) of AMLA 2001.
   
   Regulatory Citation:
   Anti-Money Laundering, Anti-Terrorism Financing and Proceeds of Unlawful Activities Act 2001, Section 16(1)
   
   Risk Assessment:
   • Regulatory Risk: Non-compliance with Section 16(1) AMLA 2001 exposes the institution to regulatory findings during BNM inspections. Failure to implement transaction threshold-based CDD triggers violates a core AML/CFT obligation and demonstrates inadequate control design. BNM has explicitly emphasized transaction monitoring as a critical AML control in Policy Document on AML/CFT Risk Management. Regulatory action could include enforcement notices, remedial action directives, or escalation to the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) mutual evaluation process.
   • Financial Impact: Potential penalties under Section 131 AMLA 2001 range from RM100,000 to RM1,000,000 for institutions, with individual liability up to RM500,000 and/or imprisonment. Reputational damage from regulatory findings could result in loss of customer confidence, increased compliance costs for remediation, and potential loss of banking license if systemic failures are identified. Costs for implementing corrective measures, conducting forensic reviews of historical transactions, and enhanced monitoring could exceed RM500,000-RM2,000,000 depending on customer base size.
   • Operational Impact: Absence of transaction threshold monitoring creates operational blind spots in AML/CFT controls. The institution cannot demonstrate compliance with ongoing CDD obligations for existing customers conducting large transactions. This creates audit deficiencies, increases false negative risk (missing suspicious transactions), and complicates regulatory reporting. Staff lack clear procedures for handling threshold-triggered scenarios, leading to inconsistent application of CDD controls and potential control failures. The Veris Engine's policy gap analysis may not flag this gap if the prescribed limit is not explicitly defined in parsed regulatory documents, creating a false sense of compliance.
   
   Recommendation:
   Immediately amend the Standard CDD Policy to incorporate explicit transaction threshold-based CDD triggers aligned with Section 16(1) AMLA 2001 and BNM guidance. Establish the prescribed transaction limit (typically RM25,000 or lower depending on transaction type per BNM circulars), implement real-time transaction monitoring logic in the Veris Engine, and define escalation procedures for threshold breaches. Conduct a gap analysis of the Veris Engine's configuration to ensure it captures transaction amount as a CDD trigger variable, not solely customer risk profile. Implement enhanced CDD procedures for transactions exceeding thresholds, including source of funds verification, beneficial ownership confirmation, and transaction purpose documentation. Update record-keeping procedures to capture threshold-triggered CDD events separately for audit purposes.
   
   Remediation Steps:
      1. Review BNM Policy Documents and AMLA 2001 Section 16(1) to identify the prescribed transaction limit applicable to the institution's business model (e.g., RM25,000 for cash transactions, lower thresholds for high-risk jurisdictions)
   2. Amend the Standard CDD Policy to add a new section titled 'Transaction Threshold-Based CDD' defining: (a) the prescribed limit, (b) transaction types subject to monitoring, (c) CDD procedures triggered by threshold breach, (d) enhanced due diligence requirements (source of funds, beneficial ownership, transaction purpose), and (e) escalation to Compliance Officer if threshold is exceeded
   3. Configure the Veris Compliance Engine to incorporate transaction amount as a CDD trigger variable in the 'Real-time Risk Scoring' module, ensuring the system flags transactions exceeding the prescribed limit and recommends enhanced CDD procedures
   4. Develop operational procedures and staff training materials outlining the workflow when a transaction threshold is breached: (1) system alert generation, (2) Compliance Officer notification, (3) enhanced CDD data collection, (4) risk reassessment, (5) approval/rejection decision, (6) documentation and record-keeping
   5. Implement automated transaction monitoring controls in the core banking system to capture transaction amounts in real-time and trigger CDD workflow alerts when thresholds are exceeded
   6. Update the Record Keeping section of the CDD Policy to explicitly require retention of: (a) transaction amount and date, (b) threshold breach notification, (c) enhanced CDD documentation collected, (d) risk reassessment results, and (e) approval/rejection decision with rationale, for a minimum of 6 years
   7. Conduct a retrospective review of transactions exceeding the prescribed limit over the past 3-5 years to identify any instances where CDD was not performed and implement corrective measures (e.g., retroactive CDD, customer re-verification, suspicious transaction reporting if warranted)
   8. Perform a validation test of the Veris Engine's policy gap analysis to confirm it identifies the transaction threshold CDD requirement and flags any gaps in the updated policy
   9. Schedule quarterly compliance audits to verify that transaction threshold-based CDD procedures are being followed consistently and that the Veris Engine is functioning as designed

3. 🟠 [HIGH] Record Keeping
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   
   Gap Description:
   While the internal policy acknowledges the 6-year retention requirement and lists specific record types, it lacks critical implementation details for compliance with Section 17 of AMLA 2001. Specifically: (1) No documented procedures for systematic record creation and storage across all transaction types and CDD information categories; (2) No specification of storage media, format standardization, or accessibility requirements; (3) No defined procedures for record retrieval, audit trails, or integrity verification; (4) No mention of how records are protected from alteration, loss, or unauthorized access; (5) No procedures for handling record destruction post-retention period; (6) The Veris Compliance Engine, despite processing regulatory documents and internal policies, contains no feature for automated record retention management, lifecycle tracking, or compliance verification of record-keeping obligations. The product focuses on risk scoring and policy gap analysis but does not address the operational infrastructure required to maintain compliant records.
   
   Current State:
   The internal policy specifies a 6-year record retention duration and identifies three categories of records to be maintained: Copy of ID, Screening Results, and Risk Assessment Form. The Veris Compliance Engine product specification includes data pipeline capabilities (OCR, semantic chunking, JSON structuring) and risk scoring features, but contains no explicit mention of record keeping, retention management, archival systems, or retrieval mechanisms.
   
   Regulatory Citation:
   Anti-Money Laundering, Anti-Terrorism Financing and Proceeds of Unlawful Activities Act 2001, Section 17
   
   Risk Assessment:
   • Regulatory Risk: BNM and law enforcement may conduct inspections under Section 17 of AMLA 2001 requiring production of transaction records and CDD information. Inability to produce complete, organized, and verifiable records spanning the 6-year period constitutes a direct violation of the Act. This is a strict liability offense with no materiality threshold—even partial non-compliance triggers regulatory breach. Additionally, Section 17 violations may be cited as evidence of systemic AML/CFT control failures during enforcement actions.
   • Financial Impact: Penalties under AMLA 2001 Section 17 violations can reach RM500,000 for individuals and RM1,000,000 for institutions, plus potential criminal prosecution. Regulatory sanctions may include license suspension, enhanced monitoring requirements, or enforcement action. Remediation costs for retroactive record reconstruction, system implementation, and compliance certification could exceed RM2-5 million depending on transaction volume and data dispersion. Reputational damage and loss of correspondent banking relationships may result from regulatory findings.
   • Operational Impact: Inability to produce records during regulatory inspections triggers immediate compliance escalation and potential business disruption. Lack of systematic record management increases operational inefficiency, manual effort, and error rates. Absence of audit trails complicates internal investigations, fraud detection, and regulatory response. Uncontrolled record proliferation across disparate systems creates data governance chaos, increases storage costs, and impairs business continuity. Inability to demonstrate record integrity undermines the institution's defense against regulatory allegations.
   
   Recommendation:
   Implement a comprehensive Record Keeping and Lifecycle Management module within the Veris Compliance Engine, coupled with enhanced internal policy documentation. The solution must: (1) Establish automated record creation workflows that capture all transaction details and CDD information in standardized, immutable formats; (2) Implement centralized, secure archival infrastructure with encryption, access controls, and tamper-evident logging; (3) Define and enforce systematic retention schedules with automated lifecycle management (retention, archival, secure destruction); (4) Establish retrieval procedures with audit trails and compliance verification; (5) Integrate record-keeping compliance checks into the policy gap analysis feature to flag retention period gaps and missing documentation; (6) Create regulatory inspection readiness reports demonstrating record completeness and accessibility.
   
   Remediation Steps:
      1. Conduct comprehensive audit of current record-keeping practices across all business lines to identify gaps in coverage, storage, retention, and accessibility relative to Section 17 requirements
   2. Develop detailed Record Keeping Policy addendum specifying: (a) all record types required under AMLA 2001 and BNM guidelines; (b) creation procedures and responsible parties; (c) storage media, format standards, and encryption requirements; (d) retention periods by record type; (e) access controls and audit logging; (f) retrieval procedures; (g) destruction protocols post-retention
   3. Enhance Veris Compliance Engine with Record Keeping Module including: (a) automated record capture and tagging from transaction systems; (b) centralized archival system with immutable storage; (c) retention schedule automation with lifecycle management; (d) audit trail logging for all record access and modifications; (e) compliance verification reports showing record completeness by customer and transaction type
   4. Implement data governance framework establishing: (a) record classification standards; (b) metadata requirements for searchability and retrieval; (c) encryption and security protocols; (d) backup and disaster recovery procedures; (e) third-party audit procedures for record integrity verification
   5. Integrate record-keeping compliance checks into Veris policy gap analysis to automatically flag: (a) missing record types; (b) retention period non-compliance; (c) accessibility or retrieval gaps; (d) audit trail deficiencies
   6. Establish regulatory inspection readiness procedures including: (a) quarterly record completeness audits; (b) sample retrieval testing; (c) inspection response playbooks; (d) documentation of record-keeping compliance certifications
   7. Conduct staff training on record-keeping obligations, procedures, and system usage; establish accountability metrics for record creation and retention compliance
   8. Perform validation testing of record retention system against Section 17 requirements and BNM inspection standards; obtain independent audit confirmation of compliance
